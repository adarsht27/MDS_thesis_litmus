Item validity in translated LLM benchmarks



Pilot analysis — MDS thesis, Hertie School
Adarsh Tripathi

Most evaluation of language models outside English uses benchmarks written in English and then translated. The usual worry is translation quality: does the translation say what the original said? This notebook looks at a different failure, one that survives a perfectly good translation.

Take item electrical_engineering/test/17 from Global-MMLU. The English asks what GAL abbreviates in digital logic design. The options are General Advance Logic, General Array Logic, Generic Advance Logic and Generic Array Logic. The question works because English separates "General" from "Generic". Hindi uses one word, सामान्य, for both. In the Hindi version, option A is character-identical to C, and B to D.

Nobody made a mistake. The Hindi is correct. The question cannot exist in Hindi, because it rests on a distinction Hindi does not draw.

This notebook detects two failures of that kind across the English, Hindi and German subsets, and reports what the detection can and cannot see.

What is being detected



Answer disclosure. The text of the correct option appears inside the question stem, so the item can be answered by matching strings rather than by knowing the fact. This is a property of the item, visible in the dataset, and is distinct from data contamination, where test items appear in a model's training data.

Option collapse. Two or more options normalise to the same string, so the item has no unique correct answer.

Each has a severity level. Disclosure counts as informative only when exactly one option appears in the stem, since an item naming several of its own options gives nothing away. Collapse becomes key corruption when the duplication involves the correct option, at which point the item is unanswerable and the answer key points at the wrong string.

Both detectors first apply Unicode NFC normalisation. Some characters can be stored in more than one way: the Devanagari letter क़ may be a single code point, or क followed by a separate nukta mark. They look identical and compare as unequal. Without this step the detectors would miss real duplicates in Indic scripts while catching them in Latin ones, which would make any cross-language comparison meaningless.

Two things to read out of this, and they are not symmetrical.

No collapses are removed. That is structural rather than lucky: translation can merge two distinct options but cannot separate two already merged, so collapse only accumulates.

Disclosures appear to fall after translation, and that should not be believed. Two unrelated languages both cleaning up disclosure is not plausible. Exact matching succeeds in English, where the stem word and the standalone option are the same string, and fails in inflected languages where the stem uses a different form. The removed column here is the detector going blind, not the data getting better.

Arithmetic, German. 7,285 ÷ 4. The English thousands comma was read as a German decimal separator, so the correct answer became a duplicate of option B and the true answer, 1,821 remainder 1, is absent from the item. Verifiable with a calculator and no German.

Economics, German. The correct option has been replaced by a verbatim copy of option A. The correct content is not in the item at all.

Digital logic, Hindi. The GAL case from the introduction.

Physics, Hindi. "Period" is rendered समय, plain "time", while the stem contains लगने वाला समय, so the correct answer is the exact word in the question. The correct term is आवर्तकाल. The translated stem also says the quantity indicates speed, which the English does not say.

All four German and Hindi items sit in Global-MMLU's human-translated tier.

The detector fires because आयो appears in the stem, but all four moons are named there, so nothing is given away, and the English item discloses identically. This case produced the informative_disclosure filter, which requires exactly one option in the stem.
 

Limitations



The detectors use exact string matching after NFC normalisation. They find a lower bound, not a rate.

They miss disclosure wherever the stem inflects a word differently from the standalone option, which is most of it in morphologically rich languages, and they miss paraphrase entirely. They cannot see failures that leave the surface strings distinct, such as a mistranslated fact inside the question. In pilot work on an Indic benchmark I found an item where the English named a Pallava king and the Hindi named a Pala king, two dynasties centuries apart; no string-matching rule will ever catch that.

Counts are therefore not comparable across languages without an estimate of how much each language's detection is missing. Producing that estimate for Hindi, by reading both flagged and unflagged items against a pre-specified coding scheme, is the next stage.
